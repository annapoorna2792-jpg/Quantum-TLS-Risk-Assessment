Post-Quantum Readiness in BFSI: Measurement and Mitigation

Two capstone projects addressing the same threat — harvest-now-decrypt-later — from opposite ends. One measures exposure across third parties an institution does not control. The other engineers key custody across cloud providers it does.

[redacted] B N · REVA Academy for Corporate Excellence · 2026

The shared threat

In plain terms: an attacker records encrypted financial data today, stores it, and reads it years later once quantum computers exist. Nothing alerts the victim, and data already taken cannot be taken back. The only questions that matter are how long the data must stay secret, and how long protection will take to deploy.

Retrospective

Every control deployed today is prospective only. Data already harvested cannot be un-harvested; the sole remedy is the expiry of its confidentiality requirement.

Silent

Collection leaves no trace at the victim. Exposure must be assessed from cryptographic posture and data characteristics, never from incident evidence.

Time-asymmetric

Collection costs are incurred now, benefits accrue later. This inverts normal risk economics and makes even low-value targets worth storing.

Regulated

In BFSI, retention periods are set by regulation — eight to twenty-five years — which makes the exposure window determinable rather than estimated.

What the evidence shows

Each finding is measured and reproducible from retained output.

Readiness is inherited, not engineered

Endpoints behind a major CDN were post-quantum capable in 83.3% of cases against 44.8% origin-served — a risk difference of 38.5 points (Fisher exact, p = 0.005). Institutions that self-host are the migration priority; the rest were migrated by their provider's default.

Confidentiality is protected, authentication is not

Most endpoints negotiate hybrid ML-KEM, so recorded sessions resist decryption. None presents a post-quantum certificate. Every institution measured remains forgeable by a quantum adversary — a Web PKI constraint, not an institutional failing.

The endpoint you assess is not the one you use

Across nine paired institutions, no API endpoint was more post-quantum capable than its own public website. API traffic is not cacheable, bypasses the CDN, and never inherits its defaults. Assessing a vendor's website systematically overstates the integration channel.

Cloud capability is not uniform

Only one of three major providers accepts key material over a post-quantum channel, and that capability is preview and software-only. Orchestration must negotiate per provider and record posture per key version rather than assume parity.

The control works and costs nothing

Under a modelled quantum adversary granted the RSA private key outright, plaintext compromise fell from 200/200 to 0/200. Key material was recovered in both conditions, which isolates the layer providing protection. Measured cost: 2.8 microseconds and 73 bytes.

Rotation is not a transaction

No two-phase commit exists across providers. Deferring activation until every import succeeds eliminated 793 consistency violations at no throughput cost — but reliability compounds: a 5% per-operation fault rate yields 43.5% rotation success across twelve operations.

Limitations, stated

Capstone 1

  • Purposive sample of 53 countable endpoints; category-level intervals are wide.
  • Only the public perimeter is observable. Settlement interfaces, terminal fleets and CDN-to-origin legs are out of reach and plausibly carry the longest-retention data.
  • Non-response correlates with WAF deployment, so excluded institutions are likely better defended — the bias runs against the reported figures.
  • The post-quantum finding rests on a single implementation; only the static-RSA result was cross-validated independently.

Capstone 2

  • The Google Cloud path was validated against a local RFC 9180 reference implementation, not the live service. Interoperability is not claimed.
  • Saga providers are simulated; fault rates are a sensitivity range, not a prediction.
  • A compromised orchestration host defeats every control. Middleware holding plaintext key material is worse than provider-managed keys, because it aggregates what providers would hold separately.
  • Of four adversaries modelled, stolen credentials is the most probable in practice; this work addresses the quantum adversary that existing key management does not cover.
Neither project claims completeness. Capstone 1 measures what is externally observable and reports what is not. Capstone 2 demonstrates a control and states plainly what it does not fix.
